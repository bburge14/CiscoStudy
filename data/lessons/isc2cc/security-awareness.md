# Security Awareness & Training

Attackers go after people because people are often the easiest way in. You can buy
the best firewall in the world, and one employee clicking one link can still let
an attacker walk past it. **Security awareness** is how an organization turns its
staff from the weakest link into a line of defense. This lesson covers the human-
focused attacks and the awareness programs that counter them.

## People: the weakest link

> Because attackers so often target people rather than technology, employees are
> commonly described as the **weakest link** in the security chain.

This isn't an insult — it's a design reality. Technical controls are consistent;
humans are busy, trusting, and distractible. A good security program accepts this
and trains people to recognize the manipulation aimed at them.

## Social-engineering techniques to recognize

**Social engineering** is manipulating people into revealing information or taking
an action. Several specific techniques show up by name, and the exam expects you
to identify each from a short description.

### Pretexting

> **Pretexting** is inventing a convincing **back-story or scenario** (a
> "pretext") to persuade someone to hand over information or access.

An attacker who calls HR posing as an external auditor and spins a believable
story to get employee records is pretexting. The core is the **fabricated
scenario** used to build false trust.

### Baiting

> **Baiting** lures a victim with something tempting, counting on their curiosity
> or greed.

The classic example: attackers scatter USB flash drives labeled "Payroll 2024" in
the company parking lot, hoping a curious employee plugs one into a work computer
and runs the malware on it. The **bait** (the enticing object) is the giveaway.

### Shoulder surfing

> **Shoulder surfing** is the low-tech act of watching over someone's shoulder to
> read sensitive information, like a password or PIN, as they type it.

In a crowded coffee shop, an attacker sitting nearby watches a victim type their
password. Defenses are simple: privacy screens, awareness of your surroundings,
and shielding the keypad.

### Phishing and its relatives

Phishing (deceptive emails) and its targeted cousin spear phishing are covered in
the Network Threats lesson, but they're squarely a security-awareness problem too,
because recognizing them is a *human* skill. Related techniques include
**vishing** (voice/phone) and **smishing** (SMS text) — the same manipulation
delivered over different channels.

## Spotting a phishing message

Training teaches employees the red flags of a phishing email. Common ones:

- A **sender address that doesn't match** the organization it claims to be from.
- An **urgent threat** demanding you act immediately or lose access ("Your account
  will be closed in 24 hours!").
- Generic greetings ("Dear Customer") instead of your name.
- Links whose real destination (on hover) differs from the text shown.
- Unexpected attachments, or requests to "verify" your password or payment details.
- Spelling and grammar mistakes, or a tone of pressure and secrecy.

The two strongest tells the exam highlights are a **mismatched sender address**
and **manufactured urgency**. When in doubt, don't click — verify through a known,
official channel.

## How to respond to a suspicious request

Awareness isn't just spotting attacks — it's knowing what to *do*.

> If an unknown caller claims to be from IT and asks an employee to read back their
> password "to fix an issue," the right response is to **refuse to share the
> password and verify the request through a known, official channel.**

Legitimate IT staff never need your password. The safe habit is always: don't give
sensitive information to an unsolicited contact, and independently confirm who
you're really dealing with before acting.

## Security-awareness programs

> The purpose of a **security-awareness program** is to help employees recognize
> and respond appropriately to security threats, reducing human-related risk.

A good program is ongoing, not a one-time orientation slideshow. It keeps security
front of mind through regular training, reminders, and reinforcement.

### Awareness, training, and education

These three words describe increasing depth, and it helps to keep them distinct:

- **Awareness** — the broad, ongoing effort to keep security *top of mind* for
  everyone (posters, reminders, short refreshers). It changes behavior and
  attention, not deep skill.
- **Training** — teaching people the specific skills to do their job securely
  (how to handle sensitive data, how to report an incident). It's role-focused and
  practical.
- **Education** — the deepest level, building broad understanding of *why*
  (think a degree or professional certification like CC itself).

For a general workforce, the day-to-day goal is **awareness**, reinforced by
**training** on the tasks each role performs.

### Phishing simulations

A common and effective tool:

> In a **phishing simulation**, the organization periodically sends its own staff
> realistic but fake phishing emails and coaches anyone who clicks.

The main goal is to **measure and improve** employees' ability to recognize
phishing — not to punish people, but to find weak spots and turn a risky click
into a teachable moment (safely, before a real attacker does it).

## Exam focus

- Employees are the **weakest link**; awareness training turns them into a defense.
- Identify the social-engineering techniques: **pretexting** (fabricated
  back-story), **baiting** (tempting item like a planted USB drive), **shoulder
  surfing** (watching someone type).
- Phishing red flags: **mismatched sender address** and **urgent "act now or
  lose access"** pressure are the biggest tells.
- When someone unexpectedly asks for a password or sensitive data, **refuse and
  verify through a known, official channel.** IT never needs your password.
- A **security-awareness program** reduces human-related risk; **phishing
  simulations** measure and improve staff's ability to spot phishing.
