# Reporting Results & Follow-Up

An audit that is never communicated changes nothing. The **report** is the
product the organization actually acts on, and **follow-up** is how the auditor
confirms that action was real. This short lesson is heavy on judgment: the exam
repeatedly tests *who does what* once a weakness is found, and the auditor must
stay in the lane of reporting and recommending — never deciding or fixing.

## What an audit report is for

> The audit report communicates the engagement's **scope, objectives, findings,
> conclusions, and recommendations** to management and the audit committee so
> they can act on the risks identified.

A well-formed **finding** is built from five elements. Learn them, because strong
findings persuade and weak ones get dismissed:

- **Criteria** — the standard or expectation (what *should* be, e.g., a policy).
- **Condition** — what the auditor actually found (what *is*).
- **Cause** — why the gap exists.
- **Effect** — the risk or business impact of the gap.
- **Recommendation** — what the auditor advises to close the gap.

The report may also carry an **audit opinion** — the auditor's overall conclusion
about the control environment — supported by the detailed findings and the
working papers behind them.

## Validate findings before you issue

A finding based on a misunderstanding damages the auditor's credibility. So the
findings are checked *with the auditee* before the report is finalized.

> Before finalizing and issuing the report, the IS auditor **discusses the
> findings with the auditee** to **confirm factual accuracy** and to **obtain
> management's response**. This is often done at a **closing (exit) meeting**.

What the auditor does *not* do with a finding is just as important:

- The auditor does **not** fix the problem personally — that would destroy
  independence and make them audit their own work later.
- The auditor does **not** hide or soften a valid finding to please the auditee.
- The auditor does **not** unilaterally escalate past management before giving
  them a chance to respond; the findings and management's response travel
  together to the audit committee.

Validating facts first is not the same as negotiating the finding away. If the
facts hold, the finding stands — but now it carries management's written
response, which strengthens the report.

## Who owns the decision

This is the single most tested idea in audit reporting, so make it automatic.

> The auditor **recommends**; **management decides and owns the outcome**. After
> a control weakness is reported, it is **management** — as the business and risk
> owner — who decides whether to **remediate** the weakness or **formally accept
> the risk**.

The auditor can disagree with a risk-acceptance decision and should document that
disagreement, but the auditor cannot overrule management. Ownership of risk sits
with the business, not with audit. An analogy: the auditor is the building
inspector who flags a cracked beam and recommends a repair; the owner decides
whether to fix it or sign a waiver accepting the risk. The inspector neither
swings the hammer nor forces the choice.

## Ranking findings and structuring the report

Not every finding carries the same weight, and a report that treats a trivial
housekeeping issue like a critical control failure helps no one. Good reports
**rank findings by risk** so management can prioritize.

> Rate each finding by its **risk** (impact and likelihood), typically as high,
> medium, or low. High-risk findings demand prompt remediation; low-risk items may
> be scheduled or, with justification, accepted. This ranking tells management
> where to spend scarce remediation effort first.

The report itself is usually structured for two audiences at once: an **executive
summary** that gives senior management and the audit committee the overall
conclusion and the most significant risks at a glance, followed by the **detailed
findings** (each with criteria, condition, cause, effect, and recommendation) for
the people who must act. Supporting **working papers** stay on file as the
evidence behind every finding, so a reviewer could retrace the auditor's work.

Reports also carry a note on **distribution and confidentiality** — audit reports
reveal exactly where an organization is weak, so they go only to those with a
legitimate need and are handled as sensitive information.

## Follow-up: did anything actually change?

Issuing recommendations is not the end. The auditor comes back to check.

> The primary purpose of **follow-up** is to determine whether **management has
> implemented the agreed corrective actions** — and whether those actions
> actually resolved the original risk.

A few principles guide good follow-up:

- Follow-up is **risk-based**: higher-risk findings are followed up sooner and
  more rigorously than minor ones.
- If management agreed to act but did nothing, that itself is reportable to the
  audit committee — the gap is now also a governance issue.
- Follow-up verifies *effectiveness*, not just activity: a corrective action that
  was implemented but doesn't work has not resolved the finding.

Follow-up closes the loop that began with risk-based planning: the auditor aimed
effort at the biggest risks, tested controls, gathered evidence, reported the
gaps, and now confirms the gaps were closed. That full cycle — plan, test,
report, follow up — is the IS auditing process in one breath.

## Keeping the report objective

Reporting is where professional ethics meet the page. The auditor presents
findings **fairly, accurately, and completely**, resisting any pressure to
overstate or understate them. A report that quietly omits an uncomfortable
finding is not a kinder report — it is a false one, and it exposes the
organization to the very risk the audit was meant to reveal.

## Exam focus

- The report carries **scope, findings, conclusions, and recommendations**; a
  strong finding has **criteria, condition, cause, effect, and recommendation**.
- **Before issuing**, the auditor **discusses findings with the auditee** to
  **confirm accuracy and obtain management's response** (the exit meeting). The
  auditor does **not** fix, hide, or prematurely escalate findings.
- **Management**, as the risk owner, decides whether to **remediate or accept**
  the risk. The **auditor recommends; management decides and owns** it.
- **Follow-up** confirms that **agreed corrective actions were implemented** and
  are effective; it is prioritized by risk.
- The report must be **fair, accurate, and complete** — never softened to please
  the auditee.
