# Audit Standards, Independence & Risk-Based Planning

Before an IS auditor tests a single control, three things must already be in
place: the *authority* to do the work, the *independence* to do it honestly, and
a *plan* that points the effort where it matters most. This lesson covers the
foundations that make an audit credible — the mandate, the professional
standards, and the risk-based thinking that decides what gets audited at all.

Throughout CISA, train yourself to answer as an auditor would: calm, evidence-
driven, and always choosing the option that best preserves independence and
objective judgment.

## The audit charter: where authority comes from

An audit function cannot grant itself power. That power is written down and
approved at the very top of the organization.

> The **audit charter** is a formal document that defines the IS audit
> function's **authority, responsibility, and accountability**. It is approved
> by the **board of directors or audit committee** — not by the managers whose
> areas will be audited.

The charter is the mandate, not the schedule. Keep these four artifacts
straight, because the exam loves to swap them:

- **Charter** — the standing *authority* of the audit function (who it reports
  to, its right of access). Approved by the board/audit committee.
- **Annual audit plan** — which audits will be done this year, driven by risk.
- **Audit program** — the step-by-step procedures for a *specific* engagement.
- **Audit report** — the communicated *results* of an engagement.

If a question asks what gives the audit function its right to access systems and
records, the answer is the charter.

## Independence and objectivity

An audit opinion is only worth something if the auditor is free of bias. CISA
treats independence very seriously.

- **Organizational independence** — the audit function should report high enough
  (ideally to the **audit committee of the board**) that operational management
  cannot suppress or steer its findings.
- **Professional objectivity** — the individual auditor keeps an impartial,
  unbiased attitude and avoids conflicts of interest.

> A **self-review threat** arises when an auditor is asked to audit work they
> previously performed — for example, auditing an application they helped design
> last year. You cannot objectively review your own work.

When independence is impaired, the auditor does not quietly proceed and does not
simply refuse without explanation. The correct move is to **disclose the
impairment to audit management** so the engagement can be reassigned or a
compensating safeguard put in place. Disclosure, not rationalization, is the
auditor's reflex.

## ISACA standards vs. guidelines

ISACA publishes its audit guidance under the **IT Audit Framework (ITAF)**. You
must know the difference between its two main tiers, because CISA tests the word
"mandatory" directly.

> **Standards are mandatory** — they state *what* a CISA-certified professional
> must do (the minimum level of performance). **Guidelines are not mandatory** —
> they provide guidance on *how* to apply the standards, and the auditor uses
> professional judgment in applying them.

The IS audit and assurance **standards** fall into three categories:

| Category | Focus |
|----------|-------|
| **General** | The auditor's ethics, independence, objectivity, due care, and proficiency |
| **Performance** | Planning, supervision, evidence, and conducting the engagement |
| **Reporting** | How results and follow-up are communicated |

Two professional obligations show up again and again:

- **Due professional care** — perform the work diligently and competently, with
  appropriate attention and skepticism.
- **Proficiency** — maintain the technical competence to do the job; if a
  specialized area is beyond you, use a qualified expert (but you remain
  responsible for the conclusion).

## Risk-based audit planning

There is never enough audit time to examine everything. Risk decides where it
goes.

> A **risk-based approach** directs limited audit resources toward the areas of
> **greatest risk**, so the engagements that matter most to the organization get
> the most scrutiny.

Planning flows from understanding the business and its risks, then selecting and
scoping engagements accordingly. Along the way you weigh **materiality** — but
in IS auditing, materiality is not only about money.

> For an IS audit, **materiality** is judged mainly by the **criticality and
> sensitivity of the information assets** involved and the **impact of a control
> failure** — not by dollar amounts alone. A weak control over a small but
> highly sensitive dataset can still be material.

### The audit risk model

CISA expects you to reason with the components of audit risk:

- **Inherent risk** — the susceptibility of an area to error *before* any
  controls are considered (some processes are simply riskier).
- **Control risk** — the risk that the organization's internal controls fail to
  prevent or detect an error.
- **Detection risk** — the risk that the auditor's own **substantive testing**
  fails to detect a material error that actually exists.

> **Audit risk = inherent risk × control risk × detection risk.** Detection risk
> is the one component the auditor controls directly: when inherent and control
> risk are high, the auditor lowers detection risk by doing more (or more
> targeted) substantive testing.

A simple analogy: inherent risk is how icy the road is, control risk is how worn
your brakes are, and detection risk is how closely *you* are watching the road.
You can't fix the ice or the brakes mid-drive, but you can choose to watch more
carefully — that is detection risk under your control.

## The auditor's mindset

Notice the pattern in all of the above. The auditor's job is to **assess and
report**, preserving independence at every turn. The auditor advises on controls
but never takes ownership of management's decisions, never audits their own work
without disclosure, and always aims scarce effort at the highest risk. Hold that
posture and most "BEST answer" questions in this domain resolve themselves.

## Exam focus

- The **audit charter** defines the audit function's **authority, responsibility,
  and accountability** and is approved by the **board/audit committee**. Don't
  confuse it with the annual plan, the engagement program, or the report.
- Auditing your own prior work is a **self-review threat**; the correct response
  is to **disclose the impairment to audit management** and have the work
  reassigned.
- **Standards are mandatory; guidelines are guidance** on how to apply them. The
  three standard categories are **general, performance, and reporting**.
- A **risk-based approach** aims limited resources at the **highest-risk areas**.
- IS-audit **materiality** = criticality/sensitivity of assets and impact of
  control failure, *not* just monetary value.
- **Audit risk = inherent × control × detection.** **Detection risk** is what the
  auditor controls, by adjusting the extent of substantive testing.
