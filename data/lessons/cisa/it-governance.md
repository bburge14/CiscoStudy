# IT Governance, Strategy & Policy

Governance answers a deceptively simple question: *is IT doing the right things,
and is the organization getting value from it?* For an IS auditor, governance is
where you check that senior leadership actually directs and controls IT — through
frameworks, committees, and a clear hierarchy of written rules — rather than
letting technology drift on its own. This lesson covers IT governance and its
accountability, the frameworks that structure it, and the strategy and policy
documents that turn direction into action.

## What IT governance is — and who owns it

> **IT governance** is the system by which an enterprise **directs and controls**
> its use of IT. Its primary objective is to ensure IT investments and activities
> are **aligned with business objectives and deliver value** — not to maximize
> the volume of technology or to drive risk to zero.

Governance is distinct from management. **Governance** sets direction, makes
decisions about priorities, and monitors outcomes; **management** plans, builds,
and runs within that direction. The exam insists on one accountability fact:

> **Ultimate responsibility for IT governance rests with the board of directors
> and senior (executive) management** — the top of the organization, not the IT
> department and not internal audit.

When an auditor finds that IT decisions are made purely by technologists with no
link to business strategy or board oversight, that is a governance weakness
regardless of how well the technology runs.

## COBIT and other frameworks

Organizations use frameworks so governance is systematic rather than ad hoc. The
one tied directly to governance of enterprise IT is **COBIT**.

> **COBIT** is the widely accepted framework for the **governance and management
> of enterprise IT**. COBIT 2019 organizes **40 objectives** into five domains:
> one **governance** domain and four **management** domains.

| Domain | Type | Purpose |
|--------|------|---------|
| **EDM** — Evaluate, Direct, Monitor | Governance | The board evaluates options, directs, and monitors |
| **APO** — Align, Plan, Organize | Management | Strategy, architecture, and organization |
| **BAI** — Build, Acquire, Implement | Management | Delivering solutions and changes |
| **DSS** — Deliver, Service, Support | Management | Running operations and support |
| **MEA** — Monitor, Evaluate, Assess | Management | Checking performance and compliance |

Do not confuse COBIT with its neighbors — a classic distractor set:

- **COBIT** → governance and management of enterprise IT.
- **ITIL** → IT **service management** (running services).
- **TOGAF** → **enterprise architecture**.
- **PMBOK / PRINCE2** → **project management**.

Two more governance tools appear on the exam:

- **Enterprise architecture (EA)** describes the current and target state of
  business and IT and the roadmap between them, so investments fit a coherent
  whole rather than sprawling.
- **Maturity models** (such as the CMMI-style 0–5 scale from *incomplete/initial*
  up to *optimizing*) let the auditor rate how disciplined and repeatable a
  process is, and benchmark it against a target.

Governance also covers **portfolio and resource management** (choosing and
balancing the mix of IT investments) and **value delivery and performance
measurement**, often tracked with an **IT balanced scorecard** that pairs
financial measures with customer, process, and learning/growth measures.

## Strategy: two very different committees

Strategy connects IT to where the business is going. CISA draws a sharp line
between two bodies people constantly mix up.

> An **IT strategy committee** operates at the **board level** and advises on the
> strategic *direction* of IT. An **IT steering committee** operates at the
> **executive/management level** and oversees *execution* — prioritizing
> projects, allocating resources, and monitoring progress.

Think of it as *direction vs. execution*: the strategy committee (board) decides
where the ship should sail; the steering committee (executives) runs the voyage
and keeps projects on course. If a question describes prioritizing projects and
allocating budget, that is the **steering committee**; if it describes advising
the board on long-term IT direction, that is the **strategy committee**.

A sound IT strategy is **aligned with the business strategy** — the auditor's
test is whether IT plans trace back to business goals, not whether they are
simply ambitious.

## The policy hierarchy

Governance only bites when it is written down with the right level of authority.
Learn this ladder cold; it recurs across the whole exam.

> **Policy → Standard → Procedure → Guideline.** A **policy** states high-level
> management intent (*what* and *why*). A **standard** is a mandatory, specific
> rule that supports the policy (a required technology or setting). A
> **procedure** is the step-by-step *how*. A **guideline** is recommended,
> **optional** advice.

- A **policy** is broad and enduring — the constitution, not the instruction
  manual. It expresses management's intent and is the foundation everything else
  supports.
- A **standard** is mandatory and specific ("all laptops must use AES-256 full-
  disk encryption").
- A **procedure** is an ordered recipe for a task.
- A **guideline** is advisory — the giveaway words are *should*, *recommended*,
  *optional*.

> The single biggest tell: **guidelines are optional; policies, standards, and
> procedures are mandatory.** And **senior management approves** the top-level
> policy — approval from the top is what gives governance documents their force.

## Exam focus

- **IT governance** = directing and controlling IT so it **aligns with the
  business and delivers value**; **accountability rests with the board and
  senior management**.
- **COBIT** governs enterprise IT (40 objectives; **EDM** = governance, **APO/
  BAI/DSS/MEA** = management). Don't swap it with **ITIL** (service management),
  **TOGAF** (architecture), or **PMBOK/PRINCE2** (projects).
- **Maturity models** rate process discipline; **portfolio/resource management**
  and the **balanced scorecard** track value.
- **Strategy committee = board level (direction); steering committee = executive
  level (execution — prioritize projects, allocate resources, monitor).**
- Policy hierarchy: **policy (intent) → standard (mandatory rule) → procedure
  (steps) → guideline (optional)**; **senior management approves** the policy.
