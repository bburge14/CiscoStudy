# Building & Managing the Security Program

Strategy is a destination; the **information security program** is the vehicle
that gets you there. It is the whole coordinated set of people, processes, and
technology that executes the strategy day after day. Domain 3 is the biggest on
the exam for a reason: this is where a manager actually *builds and runs* things.
In this lesson we cover how to develop a program that spends effort in the right
places, and the control frameworks that give it a proven backbone.

## What a program is — and what it must align to

> An **information security program** is the ongoing, managed collection of
> activities, resources, and controls that carries out the security strategy and
> keeps risk within the organization's appetite.

A program is more than a pile of tools. It spans **people** (roles, skills,
awareness), **processes** (policies, procedures, workflows), and **technology**
(the controls themselves). If any leg is missing — great tools but no trained
people, or skilled people with no process — the program wobbles.

The exam's first and most reliable point about program development is what it
must align to:

> To be effective, the program must be **aligned with the information security
> strategy and the organization's business objectives.** The program executes the
> strategy; the strategy serves the business. Break that chain and you're just
> buying gadgets.

## Base the program on risk, not on fashion

Where should a new program spend its first dollars? Not on whatever product a
vendor is pushing this quarter. The manager directs resources to the organization's
**greatest exposures**, and the only way to know those is a risk assessment.

> A security program should be **based on a risk assessment** so that limited
> resources address the **greatest risks first.** Risk prioritizes the roadmap.

This is governance's value-delivery principle in action: you don't protect
everything equally, you protect in proportion to risk. A program built on a risk
assessment can defend every spending choice to the steering committee — "we
funded this because it reduces our top exposure." A program built on fear or
fashion cannot.

### The program roadmap

Like the strategy, the program advances along a **roadmap**: a prioritized,
time-phased plan of initiatives that moves the organization from its current
state toward the desired state. Each initiative should trace back to a risk it
reduces or an objective it enables, and should come with the **resources**
(budget, staff, skills) it needs. Managing the program means managing that
roadmap — sequencing projects, securing funding, and reporting progress — not
heroically fixing things one at a time.

## Why frameworks matter to a program

You don't have to invent a program's structure from scratch any more than you had
to invent governance. **Control and management frameworks** give you a vetted,
comprehensive structure so you don't overlook whole categories of risk.

> A **framework** provides a structured, repeatable, and comprehensive model for
> organizing security activities and controls — so the program is complete,
> consistent, and credible to auditors and leadership.

Using a recognized framework also gives you a common language with regulators,
customers, and auditors, and lets you benchmark maturity over time.

## The frameworks a manager must recognize

Learn these by *what they are for*, not by memorizing their internals.

| Framework | What it is | Use it when you need… |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------|
| **ISO/IEC 27001** | Certifiable standard for an **ISMS** | A formal, auditable management system |
| **ISO/IEC 27002** | **Code of practice** — catalog of control best practices | A reference list of controls to implement |
| **NIST CSF** | Outcome-based function model | A flexible, risk-based common language |
| **COBIT** | Enterprise IT **governance & management** | To tie IT and security to business goals |

A few distinctions the exam tests directly:

- **ISO/IEC 27001 vs. 27002.** 27001 defines the *requirements* for a managed
  system (the ISMS) you can be **certified** against. 27002 is the companion
  **code of practice** — the catalog of control best practices you consult when
  you need a reference of *how* to implement controls. If a question asks for a
  **catalog/reference of control best practices**, the answer is **27002**; if it
  asks for the **certifiable management-system standard**, it's **27001.**

> An **ISMS (Information Security Management System)** is a systematic,
> risk-based framework of policies, processes, and controls for managing
> information security — the thing ISO/IEC 27001 certifies.

- **NIST CSF** organizes everything into functions. The classic five —
  **Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover** — are the signature you must
  recognize; if a scenario names those five, it's the CSF. (Version 2.0 adds an
  overarching **Govern** function, reinforcing that governance wraps the rest.)
- **COBIT** is ISACA's framework for **governing and managing enterprise IT**,
  strong on linking IT and security objectives to business goals and on the
  governance/management split you learned in Domain 1.

The manager's move is to **adopt and adapt**: pick a framework that fits the
organization and tailor it, rather than reinventing structure or following none.
On the exam, a structured framework beats an ad hoc approach almost every time.

## Managing, not just building

A program is a living thing. Managing it means:

- **Resourcing** it — securing budget and the right skills, and deciding what to
  build in-house versus outsource.
- **Integrating** security into other business processes (procurement, HR,
  change management, the SDLC) so it's designed in, not bolted on.
- **Measuring** it — which is its own lesson, but remember here that a program you
  can't measure is a program you can't manage or defend.
- **Improving** it continuously as risk, technology, and the business evolve.

## The manager's reflex

- A program must trace back to **strategy → business objectives**, and be
  **prioritized by a risk assessment.**
- Prefer a **recognized framework** (adopted and adapted) over a home-grown or
  ad hoc structure.
- Know the framework "for what": **27001 = certifiable ISMS**, **27002 = control
  catalog**, **NIST CSF = IPDRR functions**, **COBIT = IT governance.**

## Exam focus

- The program is the **execution of the strategy**; it must **align with the
  security strategy and business objectives**, and it should be **based on a risk
  assessment** so resources hit the **greatest exposures first.**
- A program spans **people, process, and technology**, advancing along a
  **risk-prioritized roadmap** with adequate **resources.**
- **ISO/IEC 27001** = the **certifiable ISMS** standard; **ISO/IEC 27002** = the
  **code of practice / catalog of control best practices.** Know which is which.
- **NIST CSF** core functions = **Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover**
  (CSF 2.0 adds **Govern**). **COBIT** = enterprise IT **governance and
  management** (ISACA).
- Prefer **adopting and adapting a recognized framework** over an ad hoc program;
  frameworks make the program **complete, consistent, and auditable.**
